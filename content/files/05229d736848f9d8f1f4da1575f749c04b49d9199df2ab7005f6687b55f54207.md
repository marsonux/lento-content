As a teenager, Joan Didion typed out Hemingway's stories word for word.

She later said in an interview that she wanted to learn how sentences worked.

For one of the great American essayists, it's a strangely simple way to practice. But there's a reason it's simple.

## Eyes and hands learn differently

When you read, you follow the meaning. If a sentence is good, you think "that's good" and move on.

When you copy it out by hand, other things start to show. Why this word. Why the comma falls where it does. What rhythm you get when a long sentence follows a short one.

**You get the meaning by reading, but you only see the structure by copying.**

For the few minutes you spend copying, you get to think at the writer's pace. What Didion picked up wasn't Hemingway's vocabulary. It was **the way his sentences moved**.

## One sentence is enough

Copying by hand might make you picture filling a whole notebook. This habit is just one sentence.

To copy only one, you have to choose. You have to decide what was best out of everything you read today. That **choosing is half the practice**.

The hand that copies and the eye that chooses get better together.

## Try this today

**Pick one sentence**

From a book, an article, a message, anything you read today, pick one sentence that stayed with you.

**Copy it out slowly**

Paper or a notes app, either works. Just don't copy and paste. Write or type it out yourself, word by word.

**Add one line on why (optional)**

A quick "What I like about this sentence is ___" trains your eye to choose faster.

Once you've collected about ten, read back through them. You'll see **your taste in sentences** right there on the page.
